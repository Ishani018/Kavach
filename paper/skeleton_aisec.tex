\begin{abstract}
LLM agents increasingly act in the world through tool calls, but deployed
guardrails overwhelmingly evaluate an agent's language rather than the
resolved arguments of the action it is about to take. We present Kavach,
a runtime monitor that intercepts every tool call at the moment its
arguments are resolved, via a parliament of four domain-specialized
ministers --- VAULT (credentials), EXECUTOR (dangerous code), CHANNEL
(exfiltration), and NAVIGATOR (unauthorized action) --- whose verdicts a
deterministic speaker combines into Block, Escalate, or Allow. This paper
reports four contributions. First, a methodology finding: across
independent lines of evidence, deterministic parsing over resolved
arguments outperforms cosine similarity over semantic embeddings when the
attack signal is structural, moving three of four detectors to
deterministic-primary detection. Second, an evaluation across InjecAgent,
AgentDojo, and a composite corpus assembled for the two detectors those
benchmarks barely exercise (in-sample, and labeled as such, with an
independent check reported alongside it), reporting benign false-positive
and Escalate rates jointly with recall throughout rather than recall
alone. Third, a
large-scale validation against real recorded GPT-4o attack trajectories,
including a benign-trajectory control under the identical firing
criterion, so the headline detection rate has an honest baseline to be
read against. Fourth, negative results reported in full: failed
authorization heuristics, an abandoned deterministic swap, independent
generalization checks against both an author-written novel vocabulary and
an externally published attack corpus, and a benign-side gap found and
fixed by direct measurement during this work. Designed to run offline on
CPU-only hardware, no GPU required.
\end{abstract}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Positioning relative to concurrent runtime work.} MXC (Microsoft
Execution Containers, Build 2026) and ClawGuard \cite{clawguard2026} both
harden the OpenClaw runtime Kavach was originally built against. MXC is an
OS-level policy sandbox --- orthogonal to Kavach's argument-content
question, since an authorized agent can still be manipulated into
exfiltrating data within its own policy. ClawGuard's 0\% AgentDojo
attack-success rate is under hand-authored rules only, its rule-derivation
component unevaluated. DeepContext \cite{albrethsen2026deepcontext} and
PSG-Agent \cite{psgagent2026} are architecturally adjacent
(Table~\ref{tab:guardrail-comparison}); our differentiation is that
mechanism choice (deterministic vs.\ cosine) is made per detection domain
on direct evidence, with generalization reported explicitly
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}) rather than only success.

\paragraph{Plan-time authorization systems.} PlanGuard
\cite{gong2026planguard}, IntentGuard \cite{kang2025intentguard}, and
Progent \cite{shi2025progent} are the closest precedents for NAVIGATOR's
authorization question, each at the plan level rather than the tool-call
level. PlanGuard's hard-constraint stage plus LLM verifier reports 100\%
attack-blocking on InjecAgent; NAVIGATOR's analogous pinned-intent design
is instead an incomplete, three-attempt negative result
(\S\ref{par:navigator-authflow}). IntentGuard's user-confirmation-on-alert
is close in spirit to CHANNEL's escalate-not-block verdict
(\S\ref{sec:channel}). Progent's progressive allowlist reports an
AgentDojo ASR reduction from 39.9\% to 1.0\%, an upper reference point,
not a number Kavach reproduces.

\paragraph{Red-team and scanning tools.} garak \cite{garak2024}, PyRIT
\cite{pyrit2024}, and LLM-Guard \cite{llmguard2024} probe or scan at the
prompt/input boundary pre-deployment rather than enforcing at the
tool-call boundary at runtime --- complementary to, not competing with,
Kavach.

Table~\ref{tab:guardrail-comparison} (Appendix~B) situates Kavach against
13 systems on five axes, drawn from each cited paper's own evaluation
setup --- benchmark subset, environment, and threat model all vary --- so
it is a map of enforcement boundaries and mechanisms, not a leaderboard;
no number in it is directly comparable across rows. Full comparison
in Appendix~B.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Kavach demonstrates that deterministic parsing over resolved arguments
outperforms cosine similarity when the attack signal is structural: three
of four ministers moved to deterministic-primary detection, the fourth's
intent-shaped question still open past a validated taxonomy classifier.
Validating against 629 real attack trajectories surfaced both real
strength and characterized gaps: this paper's negative results are as
much a contribution as its numbers.
